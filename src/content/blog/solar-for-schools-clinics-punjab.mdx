Schools and clinics rarely come up in generic solar marketing, but they are among the better-suited buildings for it, for reasons that go beyond the electricity bill.

## The load pattern matches solar almost exactly

A school's heaviest draw, from classroom lighting, fans and sometimes a computer lab, runs during school hours, which line up closely with the hours solar generates. A clinic sees a similar pattern: reception, examination rooms and equipment mostly running through the working day. This alignment matters more than it might seem: a solar unit used directly, the moment it is generated, is worth more than one exported to the grid under net metering.

## No subsidy, same as any other commercial connection

Schools and clinics are billed under the same commercial tariff structure as any other business premises, with no PM Surya Ghar subsidy, since that scheme is written for homes and housing societies. What they do get is the commercial advantage of paying from the first unit, with no free-usage line to think around, which is a different calculation from a home's, not a lesser one.

## The clinic-specific question: what happens in a cut

An on-grid system, like any on-grid system, shuts down when PSPCL supply fails. That is a safety requirement, not a flaw. For a clinic where losing power mid-procedure is not an acceptable risk, a hybrid system with a battery bank sized specifically to critical equipment is worth the additional cost. This does not mean backing up the entire building; it means identifying what cannot lose power and sizing around that.

## A case beyond the bill

For a school, a working, visible solar installation on the roof is also something students see every day. It isn't the main reason to install it, but it is a real secondary benefit institutions sometimes value alongside the financial case.
