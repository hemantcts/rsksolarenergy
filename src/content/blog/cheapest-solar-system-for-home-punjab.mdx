For most homes, the cheapest solar is the smallest on-grid system that brings the bill to zero, installed with the PM Surya Ghar subsidy. In Punjab that is often 3 kW, which comes to {inrRange(s3.primaryNet)} after the {inr(s3.subsidy)} subsidy. If you use {F.free} units a month or fewer, the cheapest option is no system at all, because your bill is already zero.

You pay the full installed price first. The subsidy reaches your bank account {F.days} days after PSPCL inspects the system, so budget for the whole amount.

## Why a bigger system isn't better value here

Punjab's free units change the maths. Once solar brings your net use under {F.free} units a month the bill is zero, and extra panels have nothing left to save. Surplus units sent to the grid are worth far less than the ones you use, so capacity beyond the size that clears your bill mostly adds cost.

## Where cutting cost loses you money

- Panels that aren't DCR and ALMM-listed. The subsidy is only paid when panels meet both rules, so a small saving on panels can cost you up to {F.cap}.
- An inverter that is too small or badly matched. It clips output on good days, and the system never makes the units it was sized for.
- A flimsy mounting structure. The panels carry a 25-year performance warranty, and a structure that rusts or shifts in a storm costs more to redo than it ever saved.

## Cheaper routes that do work

- Get the size right first. The [calculator](/solar-calculator/) finds the smallest system that takes your bill to zero, so you don't pay for panels you don't need.
- Spread the cost. A bank solar loan turns the price into instalments; see [solar loans in India](/blog/solar-loan-financing-india/).
- Go off-grid for small needs. For a farmhouse, a tubewell room or basic backup, a small off-grid kit costs less upfront, though it gets no subsidy.
- Sort out your sanctioned load early. A system can't be bigger than it, and applying for an increase after quoting delays the job.
