Ask anyone in Mohali what January mornings are like and you will hear about the fog. It sits over the Tricity for days at a time, and solar panels feel it. They keep working through fog. They just make less.

We plan on roughly {Math.round((1 - winter) * 100)}% less than the yearly average through the foggy weeks. That figure is our own planning allowance, used when we size off-grid systems, and it is the number behind the chart below.

## Why this rarely matters on a grid-connected system

Your system does not have to match your use day by day. Under net metering, PSPCL bills you on the difference between what you draw and what you export over the billing cycle, so a weak fortnight in January is balanced by the long clear days of March and October, when output runs above the yearly average.

That is why the [calculator](/solar-calculator/) sizes from your yearly units instead of a winter day. Sizing a grid-tied system for the worst fortnight of the year would mean buying capacity that sits idle for the other eleven months.

Two things are worth knowing anyway:

- Punjab's {SOLAR_CONFIG.freeUnits.perMonth} free units are counted per bill, so a winter cycle where your solar makes less and your geyser runs more can push a general-category home over the line. [Solar with 300 free units](/blog/solar-with-300-free-units-punjab/) explains why one unit over the line costs so much.
- If your bill is already near zero in summer, check a winter bill before deciding the system is too small.

## Off-grid is the one that has to be sized for fog

An off-grid system has no grid to fall back on. If the panels cannot make the day's units in the fog, the battery runs down and the lights go out, so those systems are sized for December and January from the start, not for the yearly average.

The [off-grid calculator](/off-grid-solar-calculator/) does that for you: it takes the units you use in a day, then sizes the panels against a foggy winter day rather than an average one. It also lets you add a cloudy-day reserve to the battery, which is worth doing for a farmhouse or a tubewell room you cannot reach every week.

Hybrid systems sit in between. The panels can be sized on the yearly average, because the grid is still there, but the battery works harder in winter: shorter days, more evening load, and less surplus to recharge it. The [hybrid calculator](/hybrid-solar-calculator/) sizes the battery around the loads you want to keep running.

## Getting more out of a winter day

- Wash the panels. Dust, and the greasy film that settles in smoky weeks, cost you more when the light is already weak. Plain water in the morning is enough, and our [maintenance guide](/blog/solar-panel-maintenance-punjab/) covers how often.
- Watch the shadows. The winter sun sits low, so a water tank, a stair cabin or a neighbour's wall throws a much longer shadow in January than in June. This is worth checking at the survey rather than after installation.
- Leave the tilt alone. Changing the angle twice a year does gain a little, and on a home rooftop the gain is small enough that most owners never bother.
- Do not judge the system by one bad morning. Compare a full billing cycle with the same cycle last year.
